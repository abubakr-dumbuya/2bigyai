History

A Rice Coast Cuisine, Twice Over

Sierra Leone sits on what historians call the Rice Coast — a roughly 700-mile stretch of West Africa where rice has been the staple crop for centuries. Sierra Leone's Temne rice farmers were especially skilled at building irrigation systems, and when enslaved people carrying that expertise were trafficked to the American South, they built the rice economy of coastal South Carolina and Georgia. The Gullah Geechee people are their direct descendants. Linguists have traced thousands of Gullah words back to African origins, with speech patterns especially close to Sierra Leone's Krio.

It's not a metaphorical connection — it's a documented, traceable one, down to individual families and songs. That history is the backbone of everything on this page: a coastal cuisine built on rice, palm oil, and groundnuts, carried across an ocean and still recognizable on both sides of it.

Dishes

What to Order, and Why

A working guide to the dishes that show up again and again — what they are, how they're made, and what people argue about.

Cassava Leaf (Krio: kasada lif)

Widely considered Sierra Leone's national dish — whatever anyone says about jollof.

Cassava leaves are pounded or ground fine, then simmered slowly with red palm oil, meat or smoked fish, and hot pepper until they turn a deep, glossy green. Groundnut paste or okra often goes in as a thickener, and it's traditionally a Monday dish in Sierra Leone, the same way other leaf stews have their own day of the week. Every family has an opinion on the details — how much groundnut, how long it simmers, whether smoked fish or dried shrimp does the work. The Liberian version tends to run spicier and uses a wider range of meats — worth knowing if you've had it made both ways.

Groundnut Stew

A rich, peanut-based stew Sierra Leoneans reach for when they want real comfort food.

Built on a base of groundnut (peanut) paste, simmered with meat or fish, tomato, and pepper until it thickens into something close to a sauce. It's often served alongside — or even in the same pot logic as — cassava leaf, since both lean on the same coastal pantry: palm oil, groundnuts, and rice underneath everything.
